# 01 — Research Compendium: Youth Physical Activity, Fitness & Nutrition in India

> **Purpose:** Foundational secondary-research evidence base covering physical activity, sedentary behavior, sports participation/dropout, and nutrition among Indian youth aged 6–16. This document underpins the Problem Statement and informs age-band product decisions across the Rit plan.
>
> **Status:** Secondary research, cross-checked across multiple independent sources. Primary research (direct interviews) not yet conducted — see `08_district_validation_haryana.md` for the closest available proxy evidence.

---

## 1. Physical Activity & Sedentary Behavior

### What can be stated confidently
- Insufficient physical activity is a material public-health issue among Indian school-age children and adolescents. The exact percentage meeting activity guidelines varies markedly by study design and measurement method (self-report vs. objective/accelerometer).
- Studies using objective measurement find substantially lower activity than self-report studies. A Chennai accelerometer study found average Moderate-to-Vigorous Physical Activity (MVPA) of only **25.8 minutes/day** — well below the ~60 min/day international benchmark.
- Activity is unevenly distributed across urban/rural context, age, gender, school type, safety, and access to outdoor space.

### Sedentary behavior — a worsening trend
The **India Report Card on Physical Activity for Children and Youth** (2022, peer-reviewed synthesis of 35 sources) graded sedentary behavior:

| Year | Grade |
|---|---|
| 2016 | C |
| 2018 | C− |
| 2022 | D− |

Key findings:
- Only **25.6%** of a 1,042-participant Pune sample met the benchmark of under 2 hours/day of recreational screen time.
- Mumbai study (ages 10–15): 85% reported >120 min/day screen time. Girls averaged 218 min/day vs. 165 min/day for boys.
- Thanjavur study (Grade 8–9): urban boys 6.59 hrs/day screen time; rural boys 3.28; urban girls 4.28; rural girls 4.07 (local study, not nationally representative).
- CNNS: screen time rises from ~1 hr/day in early adolescence to ~2 hrs/day in late adolescence.
- **Important nuance:** activity and sedentary behavior are not opposites — a child can play sport and still be sedentary for most of the day (commuting, studying, screens).

### National activity estimates (use with caution — measurement varies)
| Source | Age/sample | Result |
|---|---|---|
| CNNS 2016–18 (national) | Adolescents 10–19, n=35,856 | Late-adolescent girls ~10 min/day outdoor sport/exercise; boys ~40–50 min/day (domain-specific, not total MVPA) |
| India Report Card 2022 (synthesis) | Ages 5–17 | Weighted estimate: roughly half meet the 60-min MVPA benchmark (Grade C) |
| Mumbai study | Ages 10–15, n=772 | 62% did not meet MVPA guidance (64% girls, 60.2% boys) |
| Chennai accelerometer | Adolescents, n=324 | 25.8 min/day MVPA (objectively measured) |
| Multi-site 2021 survey (published 2024) | Ages 5–17, n=992, 28 locations/5 states | Mean MVPA 82.5 min/day overall; boys 107.6, girls 57.8 |

**Note the wide spread** — different measurement methods produce genuinely different answers. Treat "what % of Indian children are sufficiently active" as uncertain, not resolved.

## 2. Sports Participation & Dropout

### The evidence gap is itself an established finding
There is **no robust national dataset** to calculate sports participation, dropout, or retention rates for children 6–16 in India. The 2022 India Report Card explicitly graded organized sport participation, active play, and family/peer support as **"Incomplete"** — not low, *incomplete*, because adequate evidence did not exist.

**Do not treat as established:**
- "Most Indian children quit sports by [age] X"
- "Girls drop out at [rate] Y"
- "[Any product] solves India's sports dropout crisis"

These are typically extrapolations, small convenience samples, or marketing claims — not national findings.

### What the evidence does support (as barriers, not measured "dropout causes")
| Barrier | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Limited school opportunity | 64.3% reported PE activity at school, but averaging only **16.1 min/day** |
| Facility access | Bengaluru: playgrounds in 65% of government schools vs. only **16%** of private schools |
| Urban safety/infrastructure | Chennai children cite missing pavements, open drains, traffic, stray dogs |
| Safety restrictions (esp. girls) | New Delhi study: 82% of parents restricted children's independent mobility |
| Air pollution | Pune: 53% called it a problem; 35% said it prevented outdoor activity |
| Social support | Active friends associated with **+32.4 daily MVPA minutes** (adjusted) |

## 3. Gender Gap

One of the most consistent, well-evidenced findings across all research pulled for this project.

| Metric | Boys | Girls |
|---|---|---|
| Pune MVPA guideline met | 45.8% | 35.6% |
| Mumbai — did not meet MVPA | 60.2% | 64% |
| CNNS outdoor sport/exercise (late adolescence) | ~40–50 min/day | ~10 min/day |
| Screen time (Mumbai) | 165 min/day | 218 min/day |
| Active school transport | 37.3% | 30.6% |

**Mechanism evidence, not just correlation:** perceived neighborhood crime was associated with **26.5 fewer daily MVPA minutes** among girls specifically (not significant for boys) — pointing to safety/mobility restriction as a structural cause, not a motivational one.

> **Note:** Team Rit made an explicit decision to build Rit as gender-neutral rather than making this the core wedge, despite it being the single strongest evidence signal in this research base. This is a deliberate, acknowledged trade-off — see `RIT_PLAN.md`, Strategic Direction section.

## 4. Nutrition

### Primary evidence source
**Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (CNNS) 2016–18** — India's first nationally representative nutrition survey (Ministry of Health & Family Welfare + UNICEF + Population Council), covering ages 5–9 and 10–19, with biomarker (blood test) data.

### Micronutrient deficiencies (biomarker-confirmed — strong evidence)
| Deficiency | Ages 5–9 | Ages 10–19 |
|---|---|---|
| Anaemia | 24% | 28% |
| Iron deficiency | 17% | 22% |
| Vitamin A deficiency | 22% | 16% |
| Vitamin D deficiency | 18% | 24% |
| Zinc deficiency | 17% | 32% |
| Vitamin B12 deficiency | 17% | 31% |
| Folate deficiency | 28% | 37% |

### What is NOT supported by evidence
- "Indian parents don't understand protein" — no national nutrition-literacy survey exists
- "Sports supplements are necessary" — evidence points to food-diversity/access gaps, not a single "protein problem"
- Protein-centric messaging generally — the actual gap is broader micronutrient diversity + affordability

### Affordability barrier (real, structural)
- 30.7% of surveyed adolescents cited cost as a reason they couldn't eat healthy food
- 11.6% cited lack of access; 10.6% cited unavailability; 15.3% cited taste preference

### Recommendation for any Rit nutrition content
Food-first, affordability-anchored, deficiency-specific (not protein/supplement-focused). Use local, low-cost staples (dal, chana, seasonal vegetables, groundnuts, jaggery, eggs where culturally appropriate) rather than implying branded products are necessary.

## 5. Parental & Social Drivers

- **71%** of respondents (LocalCircles online survey, 2021, n=8,348 — not nationally representative) said they'd support a child pursuing a non-cricket sports career.
- **82%** of parents in one Pune sample strongly encouraged their child's physical activity — but this doesn't translate directly into paid organized-sport enrollment.
- Peer effects are strong: active friends associated with +32 to +44 daily MVPA minutes depending on subgroup (stronger for girls).
- Academic-sport trade-off is a recurring, credible barrier: Karnataka study found 37% of students did no after-school exercise due to academic burden.
- Cost is a real barrier but no single national percentage exists for "dropout due to fees."
